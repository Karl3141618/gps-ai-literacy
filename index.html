Why this matters now

Since February 2025, the EU AI Act requires organisations using AI to take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff (Article 4). This course is the GPS unit's contribution to that obligation, and a practical guide to using AI well in our work.

Lesson 1.1

A plain-English definition

Before rules and responsibilities, we need a shared understanding of what AI actually is — and what it isn't.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that can perform tasks which typically require human intelligence — understanding language, recognising patterns, generating text, or making predictions.

The AI tools most DRC staff encounter today are called Large Language Models, or LLMs — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot. They are trained on enormous amounts of text and learned to generate fluent, coherent language in response to your inputs.

In plain terms

You write something. The AI predicts what a useful response would look like — based on patterns learned during training. It is not thinking. It is not understanding. It is generating.

How are LLMs trained?

LLMs are trained on vast text datasets using a process that rewards generating accurate, coherent, contextually appropriate language. Through billions of examples, the model learns statistical patterns about which words and ideas tend to follow which others.

This is why LLMs can seem remarkably knowledgeable — they have "read" more text than any human could in a lifetime. But they have no real-world experience, no access to current information, and no ability to verify whether what they generate is actually true.

A useful mental model

An LLM is like an extraordinarily well-read assistant who has absorbed enormous amounts of information but cannot verify facts, check their work against reality, or exercise genuine judgment. They are excellent at producing plausible text — not necessarily accurate text.

Lesson 1.2

What AI is good at

AI tools are genuinely useful — but for a specific set of tasks. Knowing where they add real value helps you use them efficiently.

AI tools work best for tasks that are language-based, repetitive, or require drawing on broad knowledge:

  • Drafting and editing text — emails, reports, summaries, policy documents
  • Synthesising large volumes of information — pulling key themes from many documents
  • Translation and language checking — identifying awkward phrasing, inconsistencies
  • Brainstorming — generating ideas, options, and frameworks quickly
  • Reformatting and restructuring — turning rough notes into structured outputs
  • Explaining complex concepts — breaking down technical or legal language
The pattern

AI excels at pattern recognition in language. It is poor at tasks requiring deep specialist expertise, verified facts, or fine-grained contextual judgment. The boundary between these two categories is where careful human oversight matters most.

Practical examples for DRC work

TaskHow AI helps
Multi-country report synthesisPull common themes from multiple field inputs, identify gaps, draft a coherent narrative
Donor report writingStructure content, improve flow and clarity, ensure consistent tone across sections
Meeting notesTurn rough notes into structured summaries with action points and owners
Policy document reviewIdentify logical gaps, inconsistencies, or unclear language in a draft
Translation quality checkFlag awkward phrasing, regional language variation, or inconsistencies
Remember

In every case above, AI produces a first draft or a starting point. Your expertise, contextual knowledge, and judgment make the output good. AI helps you work faster — it does not replace the work.

Lesson 1.3

What AI is not good at

Understanding the limits of AI is just as important as knowing its capabilities. These are fundamental to using AI responsibly.

LimitationWhat this means in practice
HallucinationAI can generate false information confidently and fluently. It may invent statistics, citations, names, or events that don't exist. Always verify facts, numbers, and sources independently.
No real-time knowledgeMost AI tools have a knowledge cutoff. They don't know what happened last week or month. Do not rely on AI for current events, recent policy changes, or live data.
No contextual judgmentAI cannot read the room. It doesn't know the political history between stakeholders, the sensitivity of a situation on the ground, or the implicit expectations of a donor. Your professional judgment is irreplaceable.
No accountabilityIf an AI-assisted document contains an error, the responsibility lies with the person who used it — not the tool. You are always accountable for what you sign, send, or publish.
Automation biasPeople have a well-documented tendency to trust machine-generated outputs over their own judgment — even when the AI is wrong. Be actively sceptical, especially when outputs confirm what you already believe.
On hallucination — a concrete example

If you ask an AI to cite sources for a claim, it may generate convincing-looking references — journal names, author names, volume numbers — that do not exist. This is not the AI "lying." It is generating statistically plausible text. The result looks exactly like a real citation. It may not be one. Always check independently.

Lesson 1.4

The human-in-the-loop principle

The EU AI Act, humanitarian AI frameworks such as SAFE AI, and DRC's own MEAL guidance all converge on the same foundational principle.

The human-in-the-loop principle

AI can assist, support, and accelerate. But decisions that affect people — about programmes, resources, beneficiaries, or staff — must involve meaningful human review before action is taken. AI is an input. You are the decision-maker.

What does "meaningful human review" mean?

  • Reading the AI output carefully and critically
  • Verifying any facts, statistics, or citations that matter
  • Applying your own expertise and contextual knowledge
  • Editing the output to ensure it is accurate, appropriate, and reflects your professional judgment
  • Taking responsibility for the final document as if you had written it yourself

Human oversight as a legal obligation

The EU AI Act requires human oversight for high-risk AI systems: providers must design for it (Article 14) and deployers such as DRC must apply it (Article 26). For DRC, which works with displaced people, asylum seekers, and conflict-affected communities, meaningful human review of any AI tool used in contexts that affect beneficiaries is a core principle, whether or not the system is legally classified as high-risk.

The sector consensus

Humanitarian guidance is consistent on this point. The SAFE AI Framework (CDAC Network, 2026) expects organisations to keep clear internal accountability for AI-related risks, never delegated to suppliers, and to treat AI output as a recommendation for a human expert whenever the cost of error is more than low.

Chapter 2

Ethics & Responsibility

Using AI in a way that reflects DRC's values and the humanitarian Do No Harm principle.

Lesson 2.1

Why ethics matters in humanitarian work

DRC works with people in some of the world's most vulnerable situations. The stakes of getting things wrong are not abstract.

DRC works with displaced populations, conflict-affected communities, and people navigating asylum processes. The stakes of getting things wrong are not abstract. They affect real lives.

The foundational principle across humanitarian AI guidance, from UNHCR's AI Approach to the SAFE AI Framework, is the same: Do No Harm. Technology must never be deployed in a way that risks worsening the situation of the people DRC serves, or that subjects them to opaque automated systems without their knowledge or consent.

The Do No Harm principle

Before deploying or using any AI tool in your work, ask: could this cause harm to the people DRC serves — directly or indirectly? If the answer is uncertain, stop and seek guidance.

This does not mean DRC staff cannot use AI. It means they must use it thoughtfully. The ethical considerations below apply to all DRC staff, regardless of role or location.

Lesson 2.2

Five core ethical principles

These five principles apply to all DRC staff when using AI tools, regardless of role or location.

PrincipleWhat it means
1. Human oversightAI must not replace human judgment where decisions affect people's lives, safety, or dignity. A human must always review, validate, and take responsibility for AI-assisted outputs before they are acted upon.
2. TransparencyDRC staff must be honest about when AI has contributed to their work. Disclose AI use in external documents, donor reports, and communications — do not hide it behind human authorship.
3. Data protectionPersonal data — especially sensitive data about beneficiaries, staff, or vulnerable populations — must never be entered into AI tools not covered by DRC's data agreements.
4. Non-discriminationAI systems can reflect and amplify biases present in their training data. DRC staff should be alert to outputs that may disadvantage particular groups, reinforce stereotypes, or produce inequitable recommendations.
5. AccountabilityUsing AI does not transfer responsibility. If you use AI to draft a document, the document is yours. You are accountable for its content, accuracy, and consequences.
Lesson 2.3

Bias in AI

AI models are trained on data that reflects the world as it is — including its inequalities, biases, and blind spots.

AI models can systematically disadvantage certain groups, languages, or regions — often without being obvious about it.

Examples relevant to DRC's work

An AI tool trained predominantly on English-language data may produce lower-quality outputs in Arabic, Somali, or French. A model trained on Western datasets may reflect assumptions about household structure, gender roles, or risk that do not apply in DRC's operational contexts.

What to do

  • Always apply your own expertise and contextual knowledge to AI outputs — especially when writing about specific populations or geographies
  • Be sceptical of AI outputs that seem to generalise or stereotype
  • When working in languages other than English, review outputs more carefully for errors, awkward phrasing, or cultural mismatches
  • If an AI output disadvantages or misrepresents a specific community, do not use it — rewrite it
Lesson 2.4

Data you must never put into AI tools

The most important data protection rule for DRC staff using AI is straightforward.

The core rule

Never enter data about identifiable individuals into an AI tool unless that tool is explicitly covered by a DRC data agreement.

This includes:

  • Named beneficiary information — case notes, registration data, vulnerability assessments
  • Biometric data of any kind
  • Safeguarding and protection case material
  • HR and staff personal data
  • Information about asylum seekers, refugees, or displaced persons that could identify them

Why? AI tools process and may store inputs on external servers. Entering personal data about vulnerable individuals into an uncontrolled AI tool is a potential data breach — with serious consequences for those individuals and for DRC's legal and reputational standing.

A simple test

Before pasting content into an AI tool, ask: "Would I be comfortable if a colleague could read what I am about to paste?" If the answer is no or uncertain — stop.

Lesson 2.5

The disclosure principle

Transparency is a core humanitarian value. DRC staff are expected to be honest about how their work is produced.

When AI has contributed to content shared externally — with donors, partners, beneficiaries, or the public — it must be disclosed. This does not mean a lengthy disclaimer. A single clear line is enough:

"This document was prepared with AI assistance and reviewed by [Name] on [Date]."

For internal documents, disclosure is not required — but honesty with colleagues about how work was produced remains a professional standard.

Disclosure templates

Ready-to-use disclosure statements for Word documents, PowerPoints, emails, donor reports, and meeting notes are available in the Safety Kit folder of the GPS AI Hub.

Lesson 2.6

The auto-complete risk

One of the most common AI-related data protection risks is surprisingly mundane.

What is the risk?

When writing case notes, emails, or registration records in software with auto-complete or predictive text enabled, AI can automatically suggest or insert sensitive data — sometimes from a different case or beneficiary — into the wrong field or document. A single wrong suggestion accepted without checking can become a personal data breach under the GDPR.

What to do

  • Be aware of auto-complete in any system handling sensitive data — casework software, email, databases
  • Always review completed forms and documents before saving or sending
  • If your organisation's tools allow it, disable predictive text in fields containing sensitive personal information
  • Treat auto-complete suggestions as drafts to verify — not confirmations to accept
Lesson 2.7

Beneficiary rights and the right to opt out

When AI is used in processes that affect beneficiaries, humanitarian guidance and data protection law set clear expectations that DRC should respect.

PrincipleWhat this means for DRC
Right to knowPeople should be informed when AI shapes decisions about them, for example in registration, assessment or service decisions (SAFE AI Framework).
Right to understandThey should be able to understand how AI is shaping those decisions. Opaque algorithmic decisions are not compatible with DRC's accountability commitments.
Right to contestThey should have a route to challenge what an AI-assisted process decides, and to reach a human. Under the GDPR (Article 22), people have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them.
Important

Displaced populations often cannot meaningfully refuse data collection if refusal means losing access to food, shelter, or legal protection. This dependency makes the obligation to respect their rights even more critical — not less.

Chapter 3

The Legal Framework

What the law requires — and what it means for DRC.

Lesson 3.1

The EU AI Act

DRC is headquartered in Copenhagen, Denmark — within the European Union. The EU AI Act applies to DRC's operations.

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the world's first comprehensive, binding legal framework for artificial intelligence. It entered into force on 1 August 2024, with obligations phasing in until 2028. Its timeline was amended in July 2026 by the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.

What is the EU AI Act?

The Act establishes obligations for organisations that develop, deploy, or use AI systems — based on the level of risk those systems pose. For most DRC staff, the most directly relevant provisions are Article 4 (AI Literacy) and Article 50 (Transparency).

Key enforcement timeline

DateWhat applies
February 2025Prohibited AI practices + AI literacy obligation (Article 4), in force
August 2025Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models (the vendors, not DRC)
August 2026General application, including transparency obligations (Article 50), in force
December 2027High-risk AI systems listed in Annex III (e.g. employment, access to essential services, migration and asylum)
August 2028High-risk AI embedded in regulated products (e.g. medical devices, machinery)
Why this matters now

The Article 4 AI literacy obligation and the Article 50 transparency obligations apply to DRC today. This course is one of the measures the GPS unit has taken under Article 4, and completions are recorded.

Lesson 3.2

Article 4 — The AI Literacy Requirement

Article 4 requires organisations using AI to support the AI literacy of their staff, in proportion to their role and the AI they use.

Article 4 establishes an affirmative obligation. Since the Digital Omnibus (July 2026), providers and deployers must take measures to support the development of AI literacy among their staff and others using AI systems on their behalf. The law does not require any specific level of literacy for each individual, but organisations must actively work on it.

What AI literacy means

The EU does not define a fixed curriculum. The AI Act defines AI literacy as the skills, knowledge and understanding needed to use AI systems in an informed way and to be aware of their opportunities, risks and possible harms. Measures should take into account staff's knowledge and experience, the context in which AI is used, and the people it affects. In practice this covers:

  • Understanding what AI systems are and how they work at a basic level
  • Awareness of AI capabilities and limitations, including the risk of errors
  • Understanding the risks and potential harms of AI use
  • Ability to critically assess AI outputs
  • Awareness of applicable legal obligations
Danish guidance

The Danish Agency for Digital Government (Digitaliseringsstyrelsen) has published guidance on AI literacy under Article 4. It stresses that requirements depend on each person's role, and suggests measures such as e-learning, communities of practice, AI ambassadors and awareness campaigns.

This course covers all five areas. Your completion is recorded as part of the GPS unit's documentation of its Article 4 measures.

Lesson 3.3

Article 50 — Transparency Obligations

Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 requires deployers to disclose deepfakes, and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest.

For DRC, this covers public-facing content: published reports, advocacy material, website and social media content. "Public interest" is read broadly (health, fundamental rights, public administration, the environment and more). Documents sent privately to donors or partners are not "published" in this sense, but the GPS disclosure rule from Lesson 2.5 still applies to them.

The editorial responsibility exemption

The legal disclosure duty for AI-generated text does not apply if the text has undergone genuine human review and a named person or organisation holds editorial responsibility for it. The European Commission's guidelines on Article 50 (July 2026) read this exemption narrowly: a quick human "check" is not enough. Keep a record of who reviewed what. Deepfakes (realistic AI-generated images, audio or video) must always be disclosed.

The financial stakes

Breaches of Article 50 can carry fines of up to €15 million or 3% of total annual worldwide turnover. When in doubt, disclose.

Lesson 3.4

High-risk AI systems and DRC

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk level. High-risk systems carry the most stringent obligations.

High-risk AI systems (Annex III of the Act) include AI used in recruitment and staff management, and AI used to assess eligibility for public assistance or in migration and asylum procedures when used by or on behalf of public authorities. These obligations apply from 2 December 2027.

Why this matters for DRC

DRC works directly with displaced populations, asylum seekers, and conflict-affected communities. AI tools used in these contexts — even indirectly, through partner platforms — may fall into the high-risk category. Whether DRC's uses fall in scope is a legal question still to be assessed. Humanitarian guidance takes a precautionary approach: the SAFE AI Framework (CDAC Network, 2026) places AI that directly affects access to assistance or protection in its highest risk tier, and says to apply the higher tier whenever in doubt.

What high-risk use requires

  • Human oversight mechanisms — a person must be able to intervene and override AI outputs
  • Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) — formal risk assessments before deploying AI with personal data
  • Logging and traceability — records of AI system use
  • Transparency to affected individuals — people have the right to know when AI has been used in decisions affecting them
Lesson 3.5

GDPR and AI — the data protection layer

The EU AI Act operates alongside — not instead of — the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). For DRC, both apply simultaneously.

GDPREU AI Act
Governs how personal data is collected, processed, stored and sharedGoverns how AI systems are developed, deployed and used
Requires a legal basis for processing personal dataRequires AI literacy, transparency, and human oversight
Applies to all personal data processing at DRCApplies to all AI systems deployed or used at DRC
The practical consequence

When you use an AI tool with personal data, both GDPR and EU AI Act obligations are triggered simultaneously. This is why the data protection rules from Chapter 2 are not just ethical guidance — they are legal requirements.

Chapter 3 summary

The EU AI Act applies to DRC today. Article 4 (literacy) and Article 50 (transparency) are the most directly relevant provisions for most staff, and both are already in force. The high-risk classification is particularly important for anyone working in programmes that affect beneficiaries. GDPR and the EU AI Act apply simultaneously.
